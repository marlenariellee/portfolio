01 / Context

About HappierMe

HappierMe, developed by Human Wisdom, is a mental-wellness and personal-development app. The experience studied in 2024 brought together topic-based learning, daily practices, journaling, a discussion forum, coaching, and events. Its breadth created several possible ways to get help, but it also made navigation an important part of the product experience.

Our graduate research asked how first-time users understood that experience. Could they find a relevant starting point, move between activities, and complete the actions that the client considered important? The goal was to identify concrete usability barriers and turn the findings into recommendations the product team could use.

My role and contribution

I contributed as a UX researcher and designer within a University of Michigan graduate team. The broader evaluation included interaction mapping, user interviews, personas, competitive analysis, Nielsen heuristic evaluation, and first-time-user usability testing.

For the usability study, I moderated one of the five participant sessions and contributed to the team’s analysis and recommendations. My later HappierMe internship built on this product familiarity, but it was a separate phase of work. I distinguish the graduate evaluation from the subsequent feature and marketing work throughout this case study.

02 / The problem

What needed to change

An app that supports reflection should help people understand what to do next. Our testing revealed moments where participants had to work out the interface before they could reach the activity they wanted. Opening prompts, topic selection, search, and menu contents did not always match their expectations.

The design challenge was to make the product’s existing value easier to reach. We focused on clearer starting points, more predictable navigation, and shorter routes through the core activities rather than proposing additional content as the first solution.

First-time users

The intended study population was ages 17–44; all participants were new to HappierMe.

Returning users

People using daily practice and journaling needed repeatable paths back to familiar activities.

Product stakeholders

The client needed findings tied to specific screens, task failures, and actionable recommendations.

03 / Approach

How I approached the work

01

Build a picture of the whole experience

Interaction mapping documented how the product’s screens and navigation connected. Interviews, persona work, competitor review, and heuristic evaluation provided complementary perspectives on user needs and interface conventions.

Using several methods helped us compare what the interface appeared to support with what people actually understood when they used it. The interaction map remains useful evidence of the app’s breadth and the number of routes a new user had to interpret.

02

Test realistic tasks with first-time users

The team recruited five people with no prior HappierMe experience and piloted the test before the final sessions. After the pilot, task wording was made more direct. Tasks covered daily practice, changing a topic pathway, contacting a coach, writing a journal entry, posting to the forum, and finding an event.

Sessions used Zoom recording and phone screen sharing. A moderator guided each participant while the team logged observations and tracked time. I moderated one session. Debrief questions explored participants’ impressions, navigation difficulties, and whether they could imagine returning to the app.

03

Look for repeated mismatches between expectation and behavior

Four of five participants were confused by the initial “continue where you left off” and “select a new topic” choices. They preferred a recognizable Home screen as an anchor. All five opened the hamburger menu expecting navigation or settings, but encountered content such as events and testimonials.

All five also expected the search bar to search the full app, including events and the happiness survey. Its narrower topic search did not match that expectation. These repeated patterns made navigation and search priorities for the recommendations.

04

Connect task friction to specific changes

Participants noticed larger actions more easily than smaller pathways such as Events. One of five attempted to register for an event that had already passed. On the forum, all five completed a post without selecting a topic; two of five described guided journaling as lengthy.

We recommended a default Home screen, clearer topic labels, app-wide search, more prominent important actions, a better-organized menu, explicit forum topic selection, and fewer journaling prompts. Preserving journal responses would also make it easier to return to an unfinished reflection.

05

Recognize what the study could and could not establish

The app changed while the study was underway, so some participants encountered features the team had not seen when designing the tasks. Long pages also exposed a gap in our moderation plan: we had not established a consistent point for intervening when someone became stuck.

Recruitment deadlines limited demographic variety. These five sessions provided useful usability evidence, but the proportions below describe this small sample, not the entire HappierMe audience. A broader follow-up study would be needed to test the recommendations across age groups and measure changes after implementation.

04 / From evidence to decisions

What shaped the direction

Each recommendation was tied to an observed interaction. The counts describe the five-person usability study.

06 / Outcomes

What the work achieved

The graduate project delivered an interaction map, research synthesis, usability findings, and prioritized recommendations covering onboarding, navigation, search, events, forum behavior, and journaling. The outcome was a specific improvement agenda for the client rather than a generalized request to make the app easier to use.

During the later summer internship, I conducted and analyzed additional usability work that contributed to Daily Check-In and Wellness Score improvements, as documented in my internship presentation. Those subsequent product contributions are shown in a separate slide viewer below. The original graduate study did not measure a post-release increase in retention or conversion.

07 / Reflection

What I would carry forward

The clearest lesson was that interface conventions are part of a user’s starting knowledge. When a search bar or menu behaves differently from what people expect, the product asks them to relearn something before they can benefit from the content.

On a future study, I would agree on intervention rules earlier, record the product version used in each session, and recruit a wider age range. Those changes would make comparisons more consistent and help distinguish a stable usability issue from a feature that changed during testing.
